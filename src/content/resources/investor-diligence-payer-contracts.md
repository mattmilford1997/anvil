We have sat on both sides of this table: raising for care companies and reviewing them. The payer contract section of diligence is short, and it is decisive more often than founders expect.

## The four questions
*1. Which legal entity holds your payer contracts?*
Strong: "Our PC, Harborline Behavioral PC, holds 22 contracts across 4 states. Here is the list." Weak: "Our billing platform's entity." The weak answer is not disqualifying, but it moves insured revenue from "recurring revenue" to "revenue dependent on a vendor relationship" in the model.

*2. What are your contracted rates, by payer and code, and when do they renew?*
Strong: a rate table and a renewal calendar. Weak: "We're paid a fee per visit by the platform." If you cannot state the rate, you cannot state the margin on the visit, and the investor will haircut it.

*3. What is your payer concentration?*
Strong: no single payer above 30 percent of insured revenue, or a clear plan if one is. Weak: unknown, because the platform aggregates it.

*4. What happens to insured revenue if your vendor relationship ends?*
Strong: "Volume on our own contracts continues; the shared-entity portion is 35 percent and migrating on this timeline." Weak: "We'd have to re-contract from zero." That answer gets a paragraph in the risk section and sometimes a term in the term sheet.

## What the answers signal
Investors are not against using a platform. They are against not knowing what you own. A founder who launched on a shared entity for speed and can show a live migration plan to their own contracts reads as sophisticated. A founder who has been on a shared entity for two years with no plan reads as someone who has not thought about the asset.

## How to prepare, by stage
*Seed.* Fine to be entirely on a shared entity. Have the plan.
*Series A.* Own PC formed, first own-contracts live in top states, migration timeline in the deck.
*Series B.* Majority of insured volume on your own contracts, rate table, renewal calendar, concentration analysis.
