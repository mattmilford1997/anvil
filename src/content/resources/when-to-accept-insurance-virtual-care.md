Every cash-pay virtual care company eventually has the insurance conversation. It usually starts with a board member asking about TAM. It should start with a spreadsheet.

The question is not "should we accept insurance." Almost every care company that scales does. The question is when. Too early and you burn runway on an ops function you cannot fill. Too late and you start a 6 to 18 month process in the quarter you needed the revenue.

This is the framework we use. Seven signals. Each one is measurable today, from data you already have. Score them, add them up, and the answer is usually clear.

## Why timing matters more than the decision

Credentialing and contracting run on payer clocks, not yours. Commonly cited numbers: 90 to 120 days per payer for credentialing when nobody is managing it, 3 to 6 months for a point solution to get you national, and 12 to 24 months to build in-house. None of that compresses because your CAC doubled last quarter.

So the cost of a bad decision is asymmetric. Decide six months early and you spend some money on infrastructure before you fill it. Decide six months late and you spend six months watching a funnel you cannot convert.

That asymmetry is why we push for a signal-based decision rather than a gut call. Start while the cash-pay business is still working, not after it stops.

## The seven signals

### 1. CAC is rising while cash-pay volume is flat

The first signal is the one founders notice last, because it hides inside a growing top line. Pull your blended CAC by quarter for the last six quarters. Then pull new-patient volume for the same period. If CAC is up 30% or more and volume is flat or down, you are paying more to reach the same people.

That pattern usually means you have saturated the cash-pay segment in your channels. The people who will pay $150 out of pocket for your service have mostly heard of you. The next marginal patient is more expensive and less likely to convert.

Insurance does not fix CAC directly. It changes who you are allowed to sell to. The insured patient who would not pay $150 will pay a $30 copay. That is a different funnel with a different conversion rate, and it is the reason the CAC curve bends.

### 2. Cash-pay TAM has plateaued in your category

Separate from your CAC, estimate the cash-pay ceiling for your category. Take the number of people with your condition, multiply by the share who will pay full price out of pocket, and multiply by realistic annual revenue per patient. In most categories that share is small. In behavioral health, weight management, and chronic care it is often under 10% of the addressable population.

If your current revenue is already a meaningful fraction of that ceiling, say 5% or more, you are no longer selling into an open market. You are fighting for share in a small one.

### 3. Competitors are going in-network

Watch the "we accept insurance" banners. When two or more direct competitors add them in a 12 month window, the market is telling you something. The insured patient is now comparing your $150 visit to their $30 copay somewhere else.

This signal is weaker than the first two because competitors make bad decisions too. But if your CAC is rising and a competitor just went in-network, some of that rise is them.

### 4. Patients are asking "do you take my insurance" at a measurable rate

This is the cheapest signal to instrument and the most ignored. Add a question to your intake flow, tag the support tickets, and log the sales calls. Then count.

Say you have 2,000 inbound inquiries a month and 400 ask about insurance before booking. That is 20% of top-of-funnel demand you are currently declining. If your cash-pay conversion from inquiry is 15%, and the insured cohort would convert at even a fraction of that, the math on the lost revenue is straightforward.

Checking eligibility at booking is commonly cited to lift insurance-funnel conversion 2 to 5x compared to asking patients to figure out coverage themselves. So the 400 people asking are not just a signal. They are a funnel you can build.

### 5. Retention is limited by out-of-pocket cost

Pull your churn reasons. If "cost" or "can't afford it" is in the top three exit reasons, and you have already tested price, you have found the ceiling on cash-pay retention.

Insurance changes the retention math for ongoing care. A patient paying $150 a session for therapy thinks about quitting every session. A patient paying a $25 copay thinks about it less. For any model where lifetime value depends on visit count, that difference shows up directly in LTV.

### 6. The clinical model bills cleanly under FFS codes

This is a gating signal. If it fails, none of the others matter yet.

Map every service you deliver to a CPT or HCPCS code. Then check three things. Does the code exist? Do commercial payers reimburse it for your clinician types? Does it reimburse for telehealth in the states you serve?

A 50 minute therapy session with a licensed clinician maps to 90837 and bills cleanly nearly everywhere. A 20 minute asynchronous check-in with a health coach does not map to anything most payers pay for. If more than half of your revenue comes from services with no clean code, you need to restructure the clinical model before you go in-network, not after.

### 7. Unit economics survive at contracted rates

The second gating signal. Cash-pay pricing is whatever the market bears. Contracted rates are whatever the payer pays, and they vary by state, by payer, and by clinician type.

Before you sign anything, see what payers actually pay for your CPT codes by state. Then rerun your contribution margin at those rates, with your real clinician cost, your real no-show rate, and the insurance-specific overhead you do not have today: eligibility checks, claims submission, denials work, and the platform or vendor fee.

Say you have 20 clinicians, each seeing 25 patients a week. At $150 cash you gross $75,000 a week. If the blended contracted rate for your primary code in your top five states is $95, you gross $47,500 for the same visits. That is fine if the insured funnel fills the schedule to 35 patients a week per clinician and your cost per visit stays under $60. It is not fine if you assumed cash-pay margins on insured volume.

Run the numbers with the platform fee and the contracted rate shown separately, so you know which one you can negotiate.

## Scoring the signals

Score each signal 0, 1, or 2. Zero means not present. One means present but weak or unmeasured. Two means present and measured.

| Signal | 0 | 1 | 2 | Gating? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. CAC rising, volume flat | CAC flat or falling | CAC up under 30% | CAC up 30%+ with flat volume | No |
| 2. Cash-pay TAM plateau | Under 1% of ceiling | 1 to 5% of ceiling | Over 5% of ceiling | No |
| 3. Competitors in-network | None | One | Two or more in 12 months | No |
| 4. Patients asking | Not tracked | Under 10% of inquiries | Over 10% of inquiries | No |
| 5. Cost-driven churn | Not a top-5 reason | Top 5 reason | Top 3 reason after price testing | No |
| 6. Bills cleanly under FFS | Under 50% of revenue codable | 50 to 80% codable | Over 80% codable | Yes |
| 7. Unit economics at contracted rates | Negative margin | Positive only at full schedule | Positive at current utilization | Yes |

How to read your total, out of 14:

1. **Both gating signals at 2, total of 9 or more.** Start now. Every quarter you wait is a quarter of credentialing you did not bank.
2. **Both gating signals at 2, total of 5 to 8.** Start the entity work and the rate research now. Launch billing when a market signal crosses to 2.
3. **Either gating signal below 2.** Do not start contracting. Fix the clinical model or the margin first. Contracting a service line that loses money at contracted rates just scales the loss.
4. **Total under 5.** Stay cash-pay. Instrument signals 4 and 5 so you have the data when the question comes back.

## Too early: the runway trap

Companies go in-network too early for one reason. Someone with influence thinks insurance is the business model, not a channel.

Here is what too early looks like. You have 6 clinicians. You have not saturated a single paid channel. Your clinical model is still changing every quarter. You have never measured how many patients ask about insurance.

If you start contracting now, three things happen. You commit clinicians to credentialing files that go stale when the clinical model shifts. You build or buy an ops function that sits idle waiting for volume. And you sign contracts at rates you never benchmarked, because you did not yet have the volume to compare.

The fix is not to do nothing. The fix is to instrument. Add the intake question. Map the codes. Pull the rate data for your top five states. Do the entity structuring work, which is cheap and slow, so it is done when you need it. Then wait for a market signal.

## Too late: the credentialing wall

Too late is more common and more expensive.

Too late looks like this. CAC has been rising for four quarters. Growth stalled two quarters ago. The board asked about insurance nine months ago and you said "next year." Now next year is here, you have 14 months of runway, and the next raise depends on showing a new growth curve.

You start credentialing. The commonly cited timeline is 90 to 120 days per payer when unmanaged. National coverage across your top payers takes longer than that in sequence. You are looking at a year before the insured funnel produces meaningful revenue, and you have 14 months.

That is the credentialing wall. You can see revenue on the other side of it and you cannot get there in time.

The only way through the wall is to not wait for your own contracts. Which brings us to the two-phase plan.

## The two-phase plan

The timing problem exists because most companies treat "accept insurance" as a single step: form an entity, credential everyone, contract every payer, then bill. In sequence, that is the 12 to 24 month path.

The alternative is two phases that run in parallel.

**Phase one: rent.** Enroll your clinicians under a professional corporation that is already contracted with the major payers in all 50 states. Your clinicians get added to existing contracts instead of starting new ones. You are billing insured patients in weeks, not quarters. The rate you are paid is the rented PC's rate, and the platform fee is shown separately so you know what you are actually clearing.

**Phase two: own.** In parallel, form your own PC, place a physician owner in a structure that satisfies state corporate practice of medicine rules, credential your clinicians under your own entity, and contract your entity with payers directly. This is the slow part, and it is running while phase one is already producing revenue.

Over 6 to 18 months, volume migrates from the rented PC to yours. At the end you own the contracts, the rates, and the payer relationships. The rented entity was a starting point, not a destination.

This changes the readiness question. You no longer have to be sure the timing is perfect, because the cost of starting phase one is weeks of setup, not a year of build. You start when the gating signals clear and enough market signals are present. If you were early, you have working infrastructure and a small insured funnel. If you were late, you have revenue in weeks while the wall comes down behind you.

## What to do this week

1. Add "do you plan to use insurance" to your intake flow. Start counting.
2. Map every billable service to a code. Note which ones have no clean code.
3. Pull [contracted rate data](/rates) for your top three codes in your top five states.
4. Rerun contribution margin at those rates with insurance overhead included.
5. Score the seven signals. Write down the total and the date.

Do that once a quarter. The quarter your score crosses 9 with both gating signals at 2 is the quarter you start.
