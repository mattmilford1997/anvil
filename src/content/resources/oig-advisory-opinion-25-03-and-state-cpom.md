If you have evaluated a telehealth billing platform in the last year, someone has cited "the OIG opinion." It is a real and useful document. It is also narrower than the way it gets used in sales conversations.

## What the opinion says
Advisory Opinion 25-03, issued June 2025, reviewed a proposed arrangement with four kinds of parties: a management services organization and a physician-owned professional corporation (the requestors), and third-party telehealth platforms consisting of MSOs and their affiliated PCs. The requestor PC held a large number of payer contracts. Under the proposal, the requestor PC would lease clinicians from the platform PCs at a fixed hourly rate based on licensure, and would buy administrative services (marketing, scheduling, technology, billing support) from the platform MSOs for a fixed administrative fee. The leased clinicians would treat patients covered by the requestor PC's contracts.

OIG concluded the arrangement implicates the federal Anti-Kickback Statute but fits within the safe harbor for personal services and management contracts, because the requestors certified that all fees were set in advance, consistent with fair market value, and did not vary with the volume or value of referrals or business generated between the parties. On those facts, OIG would not impose sanctions.

Several law firms wrote it up as a roadmap for telehealth staffing and MSO models. That is fair.

## What it does not say
*It is federal only.* The AKS applies to federal health care program business. The opinion's analysis is about federal fraud and abuse law. Commercial-only arrangements have state analogues to worry about, and the opinion does not speak to them.

*It does not address corporate practice of medicine.* Whether a non-clinician-owned company may own or control a practice, employ clinicians, or share in professional fees is a state law question. It varies from strict (some states) to permissive (others), with fee-splitting rules layered on. The opinion is explicitly silent on this, and multiple firms flagged the gap in their analyses.

*It binds only the requestor.* An advisory opinion protects the party that asked for it, on the facts it certified. A different company using a similar structure gets useful guidance about how OIG thinks, not protection.

*It is fact-specific.* Fixed hourly lease fees by licensure, fixed admin fees, no volume linkage. Change the facts (a percentage-of-collections fee, a fee that scales with visits) and the analysis changes.

## What a compliant structure needs
Two layers, built together.

*Federal layer.* Fees set in advance in writing, supported by fair market value analysis, not tied to referral volume or value, in agreements that meet the safe harbor's terms. This is the discipline the opinion validates. It is worth adopting whether or not you bill federal programs, because it is also good hygiene under state anti-kickback and fee-splitting laws.

*State layer.* For each state where care is delivered: which entity type may deliver it, who may own that entity, whether the management fee structure is permitted, what the physician owner's actual role must be, and any registration or licensure requirements for the entity. This is not one analysis. It is fifty, and it is the analysis that most "we have an OIG opinion" conversations skip.

## How to evaluate a platform's compliance claims
Ask four questions: Is your structure built to the safe harbor's fee discipline? Which states have you done a CPOM analysis for, and can we see the summary? Who owns the PC that holds the contracts, and what is their actual role? What changes if a state tightens its rules (as several have moved to do)?
