Every virtual care company that accepts insurance has a professional corporation somewhere in its structure. The question is whose.

If you signed with a network rental vendor to get in-network fast, the answer is "theirs." You may not have thought of that as a decision. It was a line in a services agreement, and the agreement got you billing in weeks instead of months, and that was the point. But it set the answer to a question that matters more later: who owns the payer contracts your revenue runs through.

This article explains the two structures plainly, compares them across the dimensions that matter, and makes the case for doing both in sequence.

## The two structures

### Renting

Your company is a management services organization. It employs or contracts the clinicians, runs the product, handles marketing, and does everything except practice medicine.

The vendor operates a professional corporation. That PC is owned by a physician, contracted with payers across the states you need, and enrolled with those payers as a billing entity.

Your clinicians are leased to the vendor's PC. The lease is priced at a fixed fair-market-value rate, typically per clinician per month or per completed visit. The vendor's PC bills the payer at its contracted rate, collects, and pays you the lease fee. The difference between what the payer paid and what you received stays with the vendor.

You provide services to the vendor's PC. The vendor's PC holds the contracts. You see a fee.

### Owning

You form your own professional corporation. A licensed physician owns it. That physician is "friendly" in the sense that a set of agreements (a stock transfer restriction, a continuity agreement, a management services agreement) aligns the PC with your company while keeping clinical decisions with the physician.

Your MSO signs a management services agreement with your PC. The MSO provides non-clinical services and is paid a fee structured to fit the federal personal services safe harbor. Your PC employs or contracts the clinicians, gets credentialed, contracts directly with payers, and bills.

Your PC holds the contracts. You see the contracted rate for every code, by payer, by state. The rate is yours to negotiate and yours to keep.

## What actually differs

Both structures can get you in-network. Both can be compliant. The differences show up over time and under scrutiny.

### Who owns the contracts

Renting: the vendor. Owning: your PC. This sounds like a formality until you try to renegotiate a rate, add a product line, or leave.

### Who sees the rate

Renting: the vendor sees what the payer pays. You see a lease fee. If the lease is $85 per visit and the payer pays $130 for that code in that state, you do not know the second number unless the vendor tells you. Owning: you see $130, and you see what your platform costs separately. Anvil publishes both numbers side by side at [/rates](/rates) so you can compare before you sign.

### What happens when you leave

Renting: your clinicians come off the vendor's roster. The contracts stay. You start credentialing from zero under whatever entity you build next, and the commonly cited 90-120 days per payer applies. Your insured revenue drops to nothing for the duration unless you overlap the two vendors, which means paying two fees. Owning: the contracts are yours. Switching vendors for credentialing or billing services does not touch the underlying payer relationship.

### What an investor sees

In diligence, someone will trace where insured revenue actually flows. Renting: revenue runs through a vendor entity, the vendor's contracts, the vendor's tax ID. Your books show a lease fee from a third party. The payer contracts are not on your balance sheet and are not yours to assign. Owning: insured revenue runs through your PC, with a management fee to your MSO documented under a safe-harbor structure. The contracts are an asset of the group. That is a materially different picture, and the difference tends to show up in valuation and in how much time the transaction spends in legal.

### Vendor concentration risk

Renting: one vendor, one PC, one set of contracts. If that vendor changes pricing, gets acquired, has a compliance problem with a payer, or loses a physician owner, your entire insured book is exposed at once. Owning: your risk is spread across your own payer contracts, and the services layer (credentialing, RCM, eligibility) can be swapped without disturbing the contracts.

### State corporate practice of medicine

Two separate analyses, often confused.

The federal Anti-Kickback Statute personal services safe harbor governs how your MSO gets paid by a PC that bills federal programs. It cares about fair market value, fixed compensation set in advance, and a written agreement of at least a year.

State corporate practice of medicine rules govern who may own and control a clinical entity in each state. Some states are strict, some are permissive, and several have specific rules about what a management agreement can and cannot control. A structure that satisfies the federal safe harbor can still fail a strict-CPOM state's ownership rules, and vice versa.

Renting shifts the state CPOM analysis onto the vendor's entity, which is one reason it is fast. Owning means your PC has to be structured for every state you operate in. That is real work, and it is why Anvil's compliance layer does both: the [safe-harbor structure and the state CPOM structuring](/platform/compliance) together, as one deliverable.

### Rent vs own at a glance

| Dimension | Renting a vendor PC | Owning your own PC |
|---|---|---|
| Who holds payer contracts | Vendor's PC | Your PC |
| What you see | Fixed lease fee | Contracted rate, platform fee shown separately |
| Time to first billable claim | Weeks (roster-add to existing contracts) | Months (credential and contract from scratch) |
| What you keep when you leave | Nothing; re-credential from zero | The contracts, rates, and payer relationships |
| How diligence reads it | Insured revenue through a third-party entity | Insured revenue through your own entity |
| Concentration risk | One vendor, one PC, one contract set | Spread across your own contracts; services layer swappable |
| Compliance work on you | Lease agreement, FMV support | MSA, safe-harbor structure, state CPOM structuring, physician owner |
| Upfront cost | Low | Fixed, published: $15,000 formation, $3,000 per month physician owner |

Neither column is wrong. The left column is faster and cheaper to start. The right column is what you want to be holding in year two.

## Do both, in sequence

The mistake is treating this as a fork. It is a sequence.

1. **Rent for speed.** On day one, enroll your clinicians under Anvil's already-contracted 50-state PC. They are roster-adds to existing contracts, so they bill in weeks. Your insured revenue starts now.
2. **Own for the asset.** In the same month, form your own PC. Place a friendly physician owner. Start credentialing your clinicians under your entity and contracting your entity with payers.
3. **Migrate with no gap.** As each of your own contracts goes live, move that payer's volume from Anvil's PC to yours. The rented PC keeps billing everything that has not migrated yet. At no point does a patient population go out of network.

The costs on the ownership side are fixed and published. Through Foundry PC, PC formation is $15,000 one-time and the physician PC owner is $3,000 per month. There is no percentage of revenue attached to owning your entity. See [/pricing](/pricing) for the full breakdown.

### A month-by-month sketch

Timelines vary by state mix and payer mix. Treat this as a shape, not a promise.

**Month 1.** Clinicians enroll under Anvil's PC and begin billing as roster-adds. In parallel: your PC is formed, the physician owner is placed, the MSA and ancillary agreements are executed, and the entity gets its NPI and tax ID. Applications for your clinicians under your entity are prepped and filed.

**Months 1-4.** Primary source verification and payer processing run on your clinicians under your entity. Your entity's contract applications go to payers. Anvil's PC carries all insured volume. Your team watches the credentialing queue weekly.

**Months 4-8.** First own contracts go live, usually with the payers that move fastest in your largest states. For each one, volume for that payer migrates to your PC. Eligibility, coding, and claims run identically on both entities, so nothing changes for patients or clinicians. Anvil's PC still carries the payers that are not yet contracted.

**Months 8-12.** The slower payers and stricter-CPOM states come online under your entity. By month 12, the majority of your insured volume is running on your own contracts. Your rate sheet is now your own, negotiated by you.

**Months 12-18.** The tail migrates. Anvil's PC winds down to zero, or stays on as a stopgap when you open a new state or launch a new product line and need to bill there before your own contract is live. You own the contracts, the rates, and the payer relationships. The services layer stays or goes on its own merits.

### What you have at month 18

An entity you control, contracted directly with the payers your patients use, with a physician owner on a fixed fee and a management agreement structured for both the federal safe harbor and the states you operate in. Revenue that a buyer can trace to your own tax ID. And no single vendor whose contracts you depend on.

That is the outcome. The rental was how you got there without losing a year.
