You hired a clinician. They started on the first of the month. They are onboarded, trained, and sitting in your scheduling system with an open calendar. They cannot see an insured patient because the payer has not finished enrolling them.

Most founders track this as a project status. It is a cost. It accrues every day, it is larger than salary, and it is measurable with a calculator and three inputs. This article gives you the formula, shows where the days actually go, and tells you which of those days you can take back.

## The formula

The cost of an idle credentialed clinician has two parts.

1. **What you pay them.** Salary, benefits, payroll tax, malpractice, licensing, and the software seat. Call it fully loaded pay. Divide by working days.
2. **What they would have billed.** Expected weekly insured visits, times the contracted rate, divided by working days.

Add them. That is the daily cost of delay. Multiply by days from start date to first billable claim.

Two things people get wrong. First, they count only salary and treat the lost revenue as "not real." It is real. You hired for that revenue. It was in the model. Second, they count lost revenue at cash-pay rates or at list price. Use the contracted rate, because that is what the claim actually pays.

### Worked example: a therapist

Say you have a licensed therapist. Hypothetical numbers throughout.

- Fully loaded pay: $95,000 a year. At 250 working days, that is $380 a day.
- Expected volume once ramped: 25 billable sessions a week.
- Contracted rate for the primary code: $120.

Weekly insured revenue: 25 x $120 = $3,000. Daily, on a five-day week: $600.

Daily cost of delay: $380 + $600 = $980.

Monthly (21 working days): about $20,600.

A 90-day unmanaged enrollment, roughly 64 working days, costs about $62,700 for one therapist. Half of that is money you paid out. The other half is money that never came in.

### Worked example: a psychiatric NP

Now a psychiatric nurse practitioner.

- Fully loaded pay: $160,000 a year. About $640 a day.
- Expected volume: 30 visits a week.
- Contracted rate blended across new and follow-up codes: $150.

Weekly insured revenue: 30 x $150 = $4,500. Daily: $900.

Daily cost of delay: $640 + $900 = $1,540.

Monthly: about $32,300.

A 120-day enrollment, roughly 85 working days, costs about $130,900. For one NP.

### Scale it

Say you have 20 clinicians starting over a quarter, split evenly between those two profiles. Ten therapists at $980 a day and ten NPs at $1,540 a day is $25,200 a day across the cohort. Every week you shave off average time-to-first-claim is worth about $126,000. Every week you add costs the same.

That is why the idle counter matters. Not because the arithmetic is hard, but because nobody looks at it as a number. Once it is a number, it gets managed.

## Where the days go

Unmanaged, enrollment is commonly cited at 90-120 days per payer. That range is a blend of five distinct stages. They do not have equal weight, and they are not equally controllable.

### 1. Application prep

Collecting licenses, DEA, board certifications, malpractice face sheets, work history with no unexplained gaps, CAQH attestation, NPI, and the payer-specific forms. If a document is missing or stale, the file sits. This stage runs from a few days to several weeks depending entirely on how organized you are.

### 2. Primary source verification

The payer or its delegate confirms each credential with the issuing source. This is mostly mechanical. It goes faster when the application is clean, because every discrepancy triggers a query and a wait.

### 3. Payer processing and committee

The file enters the payer's queue, then goes to a credentialing committee that meets on a schedule, often monthly. Miss the cycle by one day and you wait for the next one. This is the biggest block of days and the one you influence least.

### 4. Follow-up cadence

Files stall. A payer requests something, the request lands in an inbox nobody checks, and three weeks pass. The gap between "payer asked" and "you responded" is entirely yours.

### 5. Roster-add vs full enrollment

If your entity already has a contract with the payer, the clinician is added to an existing group roster. If not, the payer has to credential the clinician and contract with your entity, which means negotiation, legal review, and loading a fee schedule. Full enrollment can double the timeline.

### Which days are yours

| Stage | Typical share of timeline | Controllable? | What controls it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Application prep | 10-25% | Yes | Document checklist, CAQH kept current, one owner per file |
| Primary source verification | 10-15% | Partly | Clean application with no discrepancies |
| Payer processing and committee | 40-60% | Mostly no | Payer queue depth, committee calendar |
| Follow-up cadence | 10-20% | Yes | Weekly touch on every open file, logged |
| Roster-add vs full enrollment | Can double the total | Yes, structurally | Whether a contract already exists |

Read the last row again. The single largest controllable variable is not how fast your credentialing coordinator works. It is whether a contract already exists for the clinician to join.

### Three moves that take days back

Before you change your structure, tighten the days that are already yours.

1. **Start the file before the start date.** Most companies begin application prep on day one of employment. Begin at offer acceptance. A signed offer and a document checklist sent the same afternoon can move two to four weeks of prep in front of the salary clock instead of behind it.
2. **Keep CAQH current for every clinician, every quarter.** A stale attestation is the most common reason a clean-looking file bounces. It costs nothing to maintain and a committee cycle to fix.
3. **Assign one owner per file and one touch per week.** Not a shared inbox. A name. If a payer request sits for more than seven days, that name explains why on Monday.

None of this changes payer processing time. It does remove the slack that turns a 90-day process into a 120-day one.

## The structural fix: rent a contracted PC

If the entity that bills already holds contracts with the payers you need, in the states you need, then a new clinician is a roster-add. No contract negotiation. No fee schedule to load. The payer verifies the clinician's credentials and attaches them to an existing agreement.

That is what Anvil's professional corporation is. It is already contracted across 50 states with the payers virtual and hybrid care companies bill most. Your clinicians enroll under it. Roster-add timelines are measured in weeks, not the 90-120 days of full enrollment, because you have removed the contracting stage and most of the committee cycle.

Run the therapist example again. If time-to-first-claim drops from 64 working days to, say, 20, the cost of delay on that one hire falls from about $62,700 to about $19,600. On the 20-clinician cohort, that is a difference in the low seven figures over a single hiring quarter.

We do not claim a specific number of weeks, because it varies by payer, state, and how clean the file is. What we will say is that the roster-add path removes the stages that are least in your control, and that is the lever.

### Why you should still build your own contracts

Renting a contracted PC solves speed. It does not solve ownership. The vendor's PC holds the contracts. You see a lease fee, not the contracted rate. If you ever leave, the contracts stay with the vendor.

So do both at the same time. On day one, your clinicians enroll under Anvil's PC and start billing. In parallel, Anvil forms your own PC, places a friendly physician owner, credentials your clinicians under your entity, and contracts your entity with payers. Over 6-18 months, volume migrates from Anvil's PC to yours. You end up with the contracts, the rates, and the payer relationships as an asset you own.

The alternative is to let the rental become permanent. That is fine for the vendor. It is not fine for you. Read [why owning your contracts matters](/own-your-contracts) before you sign anything that does not have a migration path built in.

## What to ask your credentialing team every Monday

Whether you run credentialing in-house, through a vendor, or through Anvil, the queue needs a weekly review. Five questions. Same order every week. Write the answers down.

1. **For every clinician not yet billing, what is the date of first billable claim, and did it move since last week?** If the date moved right, you need to know why in one sentence.
2. **Which files have had no payer touch in more than seven days?** Every one of those is a follow-up you own.
3. **Which files are waiting on a document from us, and who is getting it?** Application prep is the stage most under your control and the one most often left to the clinician to "get to."
4. **Which payers are we enrolling with as full enrollment rather than roster-add, and is there a contract already available we could use instead?** This is the structural question. Ask it every week until the answer is "none."
5. **What is the total idle-day cost across the queue right now, and what was it last Monday?** One number. If it is going up, the queue is getting worse, regardless of how busy everyone is.

Teams that answer these five questions weekly do not eliminate payer processing time. They do eliminate the two or three weeks that leak out of every file through stale documents and missed follow-ups. At $980 to $1,540 a day per clinician, those weeks are the difference between a hiring plan that works and one that quietly loses money.
