Ask a founder "who employs your clinicians?" and the honest answer, at most companies, is "a professional corporation we don't own." That is not a problem. It is how the industry is structured. The problem is when the founder doesn't know whose PC it is.

## Why the PC exists
Most states restrict who can own a medical or behavioral health practice. The corporate practice of medicine doctrine, in its various state forms, says the entity delivering care must be owned by licensed professionals. A venture-backed company owned by investors cannot be that entity. So the industry uses a two-entity structure: a professional corporation, owned by a licensed physician or other clinician, that employs clinicians and holds payer contracts, and a management services organization (your company) that provides everything non-clinical under a management agreement.

Every telehealth company at scale runs on some version of this. The variable is who owns the PC.

## Renting a PC
When you launch on a vendor's platform and your clinicians bill under the vendor's contracted PC, you are renting. The vendor's PC holds the payer contracts, employs or leases your clinicians for insured visits, bills, collects, and pays your company a fee. The regulatory structure is theirs. The compliance posture is theirs. The contracts are theirs.

Advantages: speed (weeks to first insured visit), no formation cost, no physician owner to recruit, the vendor carries claims and compliance risk. For a company that needs revenue this quarter, these are real.

Costs: the asset is not yours. You cannot negotiate rates. You cannot leave without restarting. Your insured revenue sits inside a vendor relationship, which your next investor will read as concentration risk.

## Owning a PC
Forming your own professional corporation in each state you need, with a licensed physician owner under an appropriate agreement, a management services agreement between the PC and your company, and payer contracts in the PC's name. The PC employs your clinicians and bills under its own contracts. You provide the platform, the operations, and the growth.

Advantages: the contracts, the rates, the relationships, and the compliance posture are yours. At a raise or an exit, the insured revenue is attributable to an entity you control through the management structure.

Costs: formation time and expense, a physician owner to recruit and maintain, contracting from zero, and full ownership of the compliance posture. Alone, months to a year before meaningful insured volume.

## The false choice
Founders treat this as either/or. It is a sequence. The right answer for most growth-stage companies:

*Week 1.* Roster-add your clinicians to a rented, already-contracted PC. Bill insured visits within weeks.
*Week 1, in parallel.* Form your own PC in your top states. Place a physician owner. Execute the management agreement. Begin credentialing and contracting your PC with the payers that cover the most lives in those states.
*Months 3 to 6.* Your PC's first contracts come online. Volume for those payers in those states moves to your entity.
*Months 6 to 18.* Majority of volume on your own contracts. The rented PC remains as a backstop for states and payers you have not contracted yet.

At no point does revenue pause. At the end, you own the asset. Drag the timeline slider on this page to see how the split moves month by month.

## What to look for in a partner
The reason most companies never get to the second half is that their vendor's business depends on them staying in the first half. Look for a partner whose model includes forming and contracting your entity from the start, with a stated migration path and pricing that steps down as volume moves to your PC.
