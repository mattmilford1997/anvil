Contracting is the least understood of the five workstreams, mostly because founders never see it: it happens between a payer and an entity, and if the entity isn't yours, you were never in the room. Here is what happens in the room.

## What a participation agreement contains
Rates (usually a percentage of a published fee schedule, or a custom schedule by code), covered services and specialties, geography, credentialing requirements, timely filing limits, claims and appeals procedures, term and termination, and increasingly telehealth-specific language about modality and place of service. Rates get the attention. The other terms cause most of the pain.

## Why you start at rack
A payer's standard fee schedule for your specialty in your state is their default offer. A new entity with no volume, no outcomes data, and no network-adequacy leverage gets the default. It is not personal. The payer has hundreds of groups asking and no reason to pay more for one it has never worked with.

Exceptions exist: a specialty the payer is short on in a region, a Medicaid MCO with access requirements it is failing, a program the payer has been asked by an employer to cover. If you have one of these, know it before you sign.

## Rate ladders
Most payers have internal tiers. Groups move between them based on volume, quality metrics, participation in value-based programs, and network need. A rate ladder is not published, but experienced contracting people know roughly what it looks like for each payer and what evidence moves a group up a rung.

For a care company, the practical version is: your first 12 months of clean claims, low denial rate, timely filing, and any outcome or access metric you can document are the evidence. Collect it on purpose.

## Leverage, honestly
You have more than you think in three cases and less than you think in one.

*More:* behavioral health in most markets (access shortages are real), specialty programs payers cannot staff, and Medicaid MCOs with adequacy gaps.
*Less:* commodity primary care or therapy in a saturated metro, where the payer can replace you tomorrow.

## The first renewal
Twelve to twenty-four months in, the contract comes up. This is the moment the last year was for. What to bring:

- Volume by code and state, and its trend
- Denial rate and clean-claim rate versus the payer's own benchmarks
- Access metrics: time to first appointment, states and specialties covered
- Outcomes if you have them, even simple ones
- State and payer medians for your codes, so your ask has a reference point
- A specific request: a percentage increase, a move to a custom schedule for your top codes, or a tier change

This only works if the contract is yours. A company on a rented contract is not at this table.

## What to watch in the non-rate terms
Timely filing windows shorter than 90 days. Termination-without-cause periods under 90 days. Telehealth modality restrictions. Site-of-service requirements. Prior authorization on codes you bill daily. Any of these can cost more than a few points of rate.
