## The phrase hides five different things

"We can get you in-network nationally in 30 days."

Every care company that has looked at insurance has heard some version of this. It is not a lie. It is also not what most founders think it is. The phrase compresses five separate layers into one, and the layers are held by different parties, take different amounts of time, and behave very differently when you try to leave.

If you understand the five layers, you can read any vendor's pitch and know exactly what you are getting. If you do not, you will sign something that feels like ownership and find out in 18 months that it was a lease.

## The five layers, defined

### 1. The payer contract

A contract is an agreement between a payer and a legal entity. Not a person. Not a brand. A tax ID. It sets the rates the payer will pay for each CPT code, the terms (timely filing, prior authorization rules, termination clauses, credentialing standards), and which product lines it covers.

The entity that signs the contract holds it. If the entity is your professional corporation, you hold it. If the entity is a vendor's professional corporation, the vendor holds it, and you are a participant on their paper.

Getting a new contract means the payer has to decide it wants your entity in its network. That decision depends on the next layer.

### 2. The panel

A panel is whether a payer is accepting new providers for a specialty in a market. Payers manage network adequacy by geography and specialty. When they have enough psychiatrists in a metro, they close the panel for psychiatry there. When they have too few, they open it and sometimes recruit.

A closed panel means the payer will not sign a new contract with your entity for that specialty in that market, no matter how good your credentialing file is. It can also mean the payer will not accept roster-adds under existing contracts, though this is less common.

Panels open and close. They are the reason "contract with every payer in every state" is not a mechanical task. Some of it is timing.

### 3. The roster

A roster is the list of clinicians enrolled under a group contract. A group contract covers an entity, and the entity tells the payer which clinicians work under it. Adding a clinician to the roster is a roster-add. Removing one is a roster-delete.

Roster-adds are much faster than new contracts, because the payer has already decided it wants the entity. It just needs to verify the new clinician and link them. This typically runs in weeks.

This is the layer where "in-network nationally in 30 days" lives. If a vendor already holds contracts across 50 states, it can add your clinicians to those rosters quickly. Your clinicians become in-network. The contracts stay the vendor's.

### 4. The credentialing file

Credentialing is primary source verification of one clinician. Education, training, board certification, state licenses, DEA registration, malpractice history, work history, sanctions checks, references. Each payer, or a credentialing verification organization acting for it, verifies the file before enrolling the clinician.

The credentialing file belongs to the clinician. It is portable in principle, but every payer runs its own verification, so "credentialed with payer X under entity A" does not carry to "credentialed with payer X under entity B" without re-submission. Commonly cited timelines run 90 to 120 days per payer when unmanaged. Managed well, with clean files and active follow-up, it runs faster, but it is never instant.

### 5. Enrollment

Enrollment is the act of linking a credentialed clinician to a specific contract at a specific payer, so that claims for that clinician under that entity's tax ID get paid at that contract's rates. It is the last step. A clinician can be credentialed but not enrolled, and claims will deny.

Enrollment is done per clinician, per payer, per entity. Change any of the three and you re-enroll.

## The table

| Layer | What it is | Who holds it | Typical time | Can you take it with you? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Contract | Rates and terms between a payer and a legal entity | The entity that signed (yours or the vendor's) | 6 to 18 months to broad coverage, per payer and state | Only if your entity signed it |
| Panel | Whether the payer is accepting new providers for a specialty in a market | The payer | Opens and closes on the payer's schedule | Not applicable. It is a gate, not an asset |
| Roster | The list of clinicians enrolled under a group contract | The entity that holds the contract | Weeks per roster-add | No. Leaving the entity means leaving the roster |
| Credentialing file | Primary source verification of one clinician | The clinician | 90 to 120 days per payer when unmanaged | Yes, but it must be re-submitted to each payer under the new entity |
| Enrollment | Link between a credentialed clinician, a contract, and a payer | The payer, tied to the entity | Weeks after credentialing clears | No. Re-enroll under the new entity |

Read the last column top to bottom. Of the five layers, the only one you can carry out the door is the credentialing file, and even that has to be re-run. Everything else stays with the entity that holds the contract.

## What "in-network nationally in 30 days" actually means

Now read the vendor pitch against the table.

To be in-network with a payer in 30 days, one of two things has to be true. Either your entity already has a contract with that payer, which it does not, or your clinicians are being added to the roster of an entity that does. So the pitch means: your clinicians are roster-added to someone else's contracts.

That is a real and useful thing. It gets you billing insurance in weeks. But three things follow from it:

1. **You do not hold the contract.** The payer's counterparty is the vendor's entity. If the payer changes terms, it negotiates with the vendor. If the payer audits, it audits the vendor's entity. You are a line on a roster.
2. **You do not see or negotiate the rate.** The contracted rate is between the payer and the vendor. Most network rental vendors quote you a blended per-visit number that bundles the payer's rate and their fee. You cannot tell what the payer actually pays for 90837 in Texas, and you cannot go to the payer and ask for more.
3. **If you leave, you start over.** Leaving the vendor means leaving its entity, which means roster-deletes at every payer. Your clinicians are no longer in-network anywhere until your own entity has contracts and they are enrolled under it. That is 6 to 18 months of rebuilding, during which you are either out-of-network or cash-pay.

None of this makes rental wrong. It makes rental a ramp. The mistake is treating a ramp as a destination.

## Commercial, Medicare Advantage, and Medicaid are not the same network

"National" also hides that there are three different kinds of payer, and they behave differently at every layer.

### Commercial

Employer-sponsored and individual-market plans. Contracts are with the carrier, and a large carrier can cover many states under one entity, though the contract is often still written state by state or region by region. Panels are managed by specialty and metro. Rates are negotiated. This is the network most cash-pay companies picture when they say "insurance."

### Medicare Advantage

Private plans that administer Medicare benefits. Contracts are with the plan, not with Medicare itself. Rates are often pegged to a percentage of the Medicare fee schedule. Enrollment requires the clinician to be enrolled in Medicare first, which is its own process with its own timeline. Panels can be tight, and plan membership is regional. If your patients are 65 and over, this is a large share of your addressable market, and it does not come along with a commercial contract.

### Medicaid

Medicaid is state by state. Each state runs its own program under federal rules, sets its own rates, and, in most states, hands day-to-day administration to managed care organizations (MCOs). A state with four MCOs is four separate contracts, four separate enrollments, and four separate sets of rules. Provider enrollment with the state Medicaid agency is usually a prerequisite before any MCO will contract. There is no national Medicaid contract, and there never will be.

This matters most in behavioral health. In many states, Medicaid is the largest payer for behavioral health services, and in some it is the majority of volume. A behavioral health company that is "in-network nationally" with commercial carriers and has no Medicaid contracts is in-network with the smaller half of its market in those states. Ask which of the three a vendor means when it says national. Often it means commercial.

## Questions to ask any vendor

Bring this list to every conversation about network access. The answers tell you which layer you are buying.

1. **Whose tax ID is on the claim?** If it is not your entity's, you are on a roster, not a contract.
2. **Can I see the contracted rate for my top 10 CPT codes in my top 5 states, separate from your fee, before I sign?** If the answer is a blended number, you cannot see the rate.
3. **Will you form my own PC and contract it with payers in parallel?** If not, there is no path to ownership.
4. **When my own contracts are live, how does volume move from your entity to mine?** If there is no answer, the plan is to keep you on their paper.
5. **Which payers, in which states, are commercial, Medicare Advantage, and Medicaid?** Get the actual list. "National" is not a list.
6. **Which panels are closed for my specialty in my launch states?** A vendor that has done this knows.
7. **What happens to my clinicians' network status on the day I leave?** The honest answer is "they come off our rosters." Make the vendor say it.
8. **Who owns the payer relationship: the rep, the escalation path, the contract renewal?** Ownership of the relationship is what gets rates renegotiated.

## Rent the roster-add for speed, build the contracts for ownership

Here is the plan that respects all five layers.

**Phase 1: rent.** Enroll your clinicians under an already-contracted 50-state PC. This is a roster-add, so it runs in weeks. You start billing insurance in your top states right away. You see the contracted rate and the platform fee as two separate numbers, so you know what the payer actually pays and what you are paying for access.

**Phase 2: build.** In parallel, form your own PC in each state where you operate, structured for that state's corporate practice of medicine rules. Place a physician owner. Credential your clinicians under your own entity. Contract your entity with each payer, one at a time, working around closed panels as they open. This is the 6 to 18 month workstream.

**Phase 3: migrate.** As each of your own contracts goes live, route new volume for that payer and state to your entity. Over 6 to 18 months the rented rosters empty out and your own contracts fill up. At the end, you hold the contracts, you see and negotiate the rates, and you own the payer relationships. Leaving is no longer a reset, because there is nothing to leave.

This is how Anvil is built, and it is how the eleven care companies our team runs went in-network. Rent the roster-add because speed matters. Build the contracts because ownership matters more.
