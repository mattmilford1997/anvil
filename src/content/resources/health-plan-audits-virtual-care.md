We run internal chart audits for every customer billing through Anvil, and we have handled payer audits for several of them. This is what we have learned.

## How audits are triggered now
Payers run analytics on every claim. A group whose distribution of codes, visit frequency, time-based billing, or modifiers differs from its specialty peers gets flagged. Telehealth companies are structurally likely to differ from brick-and-mortar peers, which means they are structurally likely to be flagged. This is not a sign of wrongdoing. It is a sign of being different, and the payer's next step is to ask for charts.

## The three flags
*1. Code distribution.* A practice billing a high share of the longest E/M or psychotherapy codes relative to peers. Sometimes justified by the model. Always reviewed.

*2. Frequency and duration outliers.* Patients seen more often than typical, or visits that are consistently at the maximum time band. Again, sometimes right. Always reviewed.

*3. Modifier and place-of-service patterns.* Telehealth modifiers, place-of-service codes, and audio-only indicators that are inconsistent within a group or inconsistent with the payer's telehealth policy. This one is usually a process error, and it is the easiest to fix and the most common cause of recoupment.

## The five rationalizations
We hear these from founders, and each one delays the fix.

1. *"We're too small to audit."* Analytics do not care about size.
2. *"Our clinicians are excellent; the notes reflect that."* Clinical quality and documentation quality are different things. Excellent clinicians write incomplete notes all the time.
3. *"We only bill commercial, so the federal rules don't apply."* Commercial payers audit and recoup under contract, and state laws mirror many federal standards.
4. *"Our EHR template handles it."* Templates produce identical notes across patients, which is itself a flag.
5. *"We'll deal with it if it happens."* By the time it happens, the charts are written.

## The charting standard
One standard, applied to every note, audited internally on a sample every month:

- *Medical necessity,* stated in the clinician's words for this patient on this date, not a templated phrase.
- *Time,* when billing time-based codes: start and stop or total minutes, consistent with the code.
- *Modality,* documented in the note: video or audio-only, patient location (state), clinician location, and consent to telehealth where required.
- *Elements of the code,* present in the note: for E/M, the history, exam or data, and decision-making the level requires; for psychotherapy, the interventions and response.
- *Plan and follow-up,* specific enough that frequency is justified by the plan.
- *Signature and timing,* signed within the payer's window, addenda dated.

## Internal audit cadence
A random sample per clinician per month, scored against the standard, with results fed back to the clinician and tracked over time. Groups that do this find that documentation improves within one quarter and that a payer audit becomes a document-gathering exercise rather than a crisis. It also surfaces the modifier and place-of-service errors before they become recoupment.

## What to do when the letter arrives
Do not panic and do not ignore it. Gather the requested charts, review them against the standard before sending, respond within the deadline, and involve counsel if the request is broad or references a specific allegation. Most audits close with no or minimal recoupment when the charts are good. The ones that go badly are the ones where the charts were never checked.
