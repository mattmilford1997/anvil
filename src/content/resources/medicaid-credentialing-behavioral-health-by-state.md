Commercial payers are hard to credential with. Medicaid is hard in a different way: every state is its own system, and most states have handed the actual member relationship to a handful of managed care organizations that each run their own panel.

## The structure
In most states, a behavioral health group billing Medicaid needs:

1. *State Medicaid enrollment.* Your entity and each clinician enrolled with the state program (provider type, taxonomy, and sometimes site-specific). This is the prerequisite for everything else.
2. *MCO credentialing and contracting.* Each MCO in the state credentials your entity and clinicians and executes its own contract. Panel status varies by MCO and by provider type. A state with five MCOs is five separate processes.
3. *Provider type mapping.* Whether an LPC, LCSW, LMFT, psych NP, or BCBA can enroll, bill independently, or must bill under supervision is a state-by-state rule. Getting this wrong means a denial pattern that takes months to diagnose.
4. *Telehealth allowances.* Which services are payable via telehealth, whether audio-only is allowed, and whether an in-state address is required for the entity or clinician.

Three of those four are yours to get right before the first application goes in. Only the second one is a queue you wait in.

## Six states, six structures
The table below describes how each state routes behavioral health inside Medicaid managed care and the sequencing lesson that follows from it. Plan lists, provider type rules, and telehealth policy change often. Verify the current state before you apply.

| State | How BH runs inside managed Medicaid | Lesson |
|---|---|---|
| Virginia | Cardinal Care MCOs, several, each with its own BH panel | Enroll the supervisor first; resident claims deny if the supervising clinician is not on the MCO roster |
| Ohio | Next Generation MCOs plus OhioRISE for youth BH | Apply to OhioRISE at the same time as the MCOs or youth referrals stall for a quarter |
| Michigan | MCOs for mild-to-moderate BH; specialty BH through regional PIHPs | Decide which tier of BH you deliver before applying; the PIHP process is a separate contract |
| Arizona | AHCCCS plans with physical health and BH integrated | No carve-out to navigate; a good first Medicaid state for a virtual BH group |
| Colorado | Regional Accountable Entities administer the BH benefit | Contract with the RAE for your region first; the physical-health plan does not pay BH claims |
| Massachusetts | MassHealth ACOs plus a single statewide BH carve-out administrator | Credential with the carve-out administrator before the ACOs or most therapy claims have nowhere to go |
<!-- verify -->

*Virginia.* Cardinal Care is the managed care program, and a handful of MCOs each credential behavioral health separately. <!-- verify --> Residents in counseling and supervisees in social work may bill under a licensed supervisor, and audio-only telehealth is allowed for many BH services. <!-- verify --> The trap is sequencing the supervisor after the resident.

*Ohio.* The Next Generation MCOs cover adult BH, and OhioRISE is a separate statewide plan for youth with complex BH needs. <!-- verify --> Most codes require independent licensure. <!-- verify --> If you serve youth, OhioRISE is not optional and it does not ride along with the MCO contracts.

*Michigan.* Mild-to-moderate BH runs through the MCOs. Specialty BH runs through regional prepaid inpatient health plans, the PIHPs, under a carve-out. <!-- verify --> MCO enrollment does not cover specialty services, so a group offering both is running two enrollment tracks with two sets of contracts.

*Arizona.* AHCCCS Complete Care plans integrate physical and behavioral health, so there is no carve-out and telehealth coverage is broad. <!-- verify --> Of the six, this is the simplest structure to enter, which makes it a sensible first state for a virtual group.

*Colorado.* The BH benefit is administered by Regional Accountable Entities, not the physical health plan. <!-- verify --> Contract with the RAE for the region you serve. A physical-health plan contract will look like Medicaid participation and pay nothing for therapy.

*Massachusetts.* MassHealth runs ACOs and MCOs, and outpatient BH for many members is routed through a single statewide behavioral health carve-out administrator. <!-- verify --> Credential there first. ACO contracts alone leave most therapy claims with no payer.

## Sequencing for a multi-state BH group
1. *Enroll with the state program first, always.* MCOs will not process you without it, and in some states the FFS enrollment is the slowest step in the whole chain.
2. *Prioritize MCOs by member share, not alphabetically.* In most states, a couple of MCOs cover the majority of Medicaid BH members. Get those first.
3. *Find the carve-out before you find the MCOs.* In the states above, the entity that actually pays for therapy is often not the entity called the MCO. Map that before you file anything.
4. *Map provider types before you hire.* If a state does not allow a given license to bill independently, hiring that license for that state is an idle clinician. Build the hiring plan from the enrollment rules, not the other way around.
5. *Check telehealth and address rules before you launch.* Some states require an in-state service location or a state-licensed entity. These are formation questions, not credentialing questions, and they need answers before the first application goes in.
6. *Expect variance.* Timelines differ by state, by MCO, and by month. Plan cash flow for the slowest plausible path, not the fastest one you heard about.

## A worked example
Say you have 20 clinicians and are entering 2 of the states above. In the integrated state, each clinician needs state enrollment plus a handful of plan credentialings. In the carve-out state, each clinician needs state enrollment, the carve-out administrator, and then the plans. That second state is a longer chain with a hard dependency at the front. If you launch both on the same day and hire on the same schedule, the carve-out state's clinicians sit idle longer. Stagger the hiring, or start the carve-out state's applications first.
